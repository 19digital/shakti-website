FROM THE WORKS

Notes on Rice Mill Engineering

Practical notes from two decades of designing and installing pre-milling lines — sizing, process choices and what tends to go wrong.

Choosing the Right Bucket Elevator Capacity for Your Mill

Sizing a bucket elevator correctly is one of the most consequential decisions in a pre-milling line. Undersize it and the whole line throttles down to match; oversize it and you pay for capacity you never use. The right starting point is your mill's actual hourly paddy intake, not its theoretical maximum — elevators are easiest to plan for when the number reflects a normal working day rather than a best-case burst. From there, bucket spacing, belt speed and head-pulley diameter are tuned together so the elevator neither starves nor floods the next stage in the line, whether that's a pre-cleaner, a dryer feed or a parboiling tank.

Why Vertical Paddy Dryers Suit Humid Climates

In regions with high ambient humidity, drying paddy evenly is harder than it looks — a batch that looks dry on the surface can still be damp at the core, which shows up later as broken rice at milling. Vertical dryers address this by moving grain through multiple drying and tempering zones, giving moisture time to equalise between passes rather than forcing it out in one aggressive pass. The result is a more uniform final moisture content across the batch, which is exactly what protects head-rice yield downstream.

Parboiling 101: What It Does to Milling Yield

Parboiling — soaking, steaming and drying paddy before milling — gelatinises the starch inside the grain, which strengthens it against the mechanical stress of milling. Mills that parboil consistently see fewer broken grains and a higher percentage of head rice coming off the same paddy, which is the main reason the process remains standard across much of South Asia despite the extra processing step. Getting consistent results depends on holding steam pressure and soak time steady across batches — which is why the tank and steam-generation equipment need to be sized to the mill's real throughput, not scaled up casually from a smaller reference design.

Storage Silos: Sizing for Seasonal Paddy Intake

Paddy arrives seasonally but mills run year-round, so storage silo capacity has to bridge that gap without letting grain sit long enough to lose quality. A silo that's too small forces a mill to buy paddy piecemeal at less favourable prices through the off-season; one that's oversized ties up capital in steel that stands empty most of the year. The right number usually comes from mapping expected intake against planned monthly milling volume, then adding a working margin for aeration and turnover rather than treating the silo as pure dead storage.
